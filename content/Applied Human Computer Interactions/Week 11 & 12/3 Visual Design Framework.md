
####  1. Develop Experience Attributes

This step focuses on identifying the "tone" and brand identity of the product through research and stakeholder collaboration.

- **Initial Research:** Designers begin by gathering and familiarizing themselves with **existing brand guidelines**. They also collect examples of other strongly branded products, interfaces, and services to help stakeholders differentiate between various brand domains. For **example**, if designing for the automotive industry, a designer might show examples from **BMW, Toyota, Ferrari, and Tesla**.

- **Qualitative Data:** Relevant terms are pulled from user interviews, specifically focusing on **pain points**. If users describe a competitor as "unintuitive," attributes like "**friendly**," "**easy**," or "**understandable**" might be proposed.

- **Stakeholder Discussion:** The team discusses the product’s "sub brand" with stakeholders to reach a consensus.

- **Selecting Adjectives:** The goal is to identify a **minimum number of adjectives** that define and distinguish the product.
    - **Precision:** If a word has multiple meanings, the exact sense must be documented; for **example**, "**sharp**" could mean **precision and sleekness** or **intelligence and wit**.
    - **Aspiration:** Attributes should be **aspirational**—choosing "**brilliant**" over "**smart**"—and must be refined until they distinguish the brand from all competitors.

- **Finalization:** The proposed set of attributes is checked and finalized with stakeholders and marketers.

#### 2. Develop Visual Language Studies

These studies explore a variety of visual treatments to find the "hidden language" that conveys messages through human perception.

- **Visual Elements:** Studies include exploring **color**, **typography**, and **widget treatments**.
- **Material Properties:** Designers define the overall **dimensionality** and "**material**" feel of the interface, such as whether it feels like **glass or paper**.
- **Abstraction:** Crucially, these studies should be **abstract and independent** of the interaction design. This allows the team to assess the overall tone and suitability without distracting stakeholders with the details of rough interaction designs.

#### 3. Apply Style to Screen Archetypes

The final phase makes the design concrete by merging the visual style with the established interaction structure.

- **Key Screen Application:** One or two selected visual styles are applied to key screens.
- **Timing and Coordination:** This step is typically performed **close to the end of the interaction framework** when the design has stabilized.
- **Refinement and Feasibility:** The style is further refined to reflect specific **key behaviors and information**. This makes the design concrete, allowing the team to assess the **feasibility of the solution** without the high overhead of updating numerous screens for minor changes.
- **Feedback:** A highly rendered screen archetype makes it significantly easier to **elicit feedback from stakeholders**.

---

**Analogy for Understanding:** Developing a visual design framework is like **branding a new restaurant**. First, you **develop experience attributes** by deciding if the "vibe" is "rustic and cozy" or "modern and industrial." Next, you **develop visual language studies** by looking at paint swatches, fabric textures, and font styles for the menu in the abstract. Finally, you **apply the style to archetypes** by fully decorating one "model" booth and printing one sample menu to see if the chosen aesthetic actually works in the physical space before doing the whole building.
