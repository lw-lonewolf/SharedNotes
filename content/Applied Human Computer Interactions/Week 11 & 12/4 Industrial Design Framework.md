#### Core Design Principle

The central tenet of this process is that **there is only one user experience**, meaning **form and behavior must be designed in concert**. The physical appearance of a device cannot be separated from how a user interacts with it; they must be developed together to create a seamless experience.

#### 1. Collaborate on Form Factor and Input Methods

In this initial step, industrial designers work closely with interaction designers to ensure the **demands of interaction guide the industrial design**. Decisions regarding the physical shape (form factor) and how a user provides information (input methods) are foundational to the hardware's success.

####  2. Develop Rough Prototypes

Designers transition from sketches to physical models to test their ideas.

- **Materials:** Industrial designers often create **rough prototypes using foam board** and other simple materials.
- **Stakeholder Review:** These prototypes are shown to various stakeholders to address different **cost and ergonomic considerations**. This allows the team to identify physical discomfort or manufacturing expenses early in the process.

#### 3. Develop Form Language Studies

This step involves exploring the aesthetic and tactile "look" of the product across multiple iterations.

- **Aesthetic Elements:** These studies define the product's **shape, dimensionality, materials, colors, and finish**.
- **Data-Driven Design:** The chosen "look" is not arbitrary; it is heavily informed by **persona goals, attitudes, aptitudes, and experience keywords** identified during research.
- **Constraint Integration:** The design must also account for **environmental factors** (where the product is used) as well as **manufacturing and pricing constraints** (what it costs to build).

---

**Analogy for Understanding:** Defining the industrial design framework is like **designing a high-end chef's knife**. You don't just pick a "cool" handle color and hope for the best. First, you **collaborate** to ensure the blade shape matches the interaction (chopping vs. slicing). You build **rough prototypes** out of wood to see if the handle fits different hand sizes (**ergonomics**). Finally, you conduct **form language studies** to decide if the handle should be polished wood or textured rubber based on the chef's **persona goals** for grip and professional style.
