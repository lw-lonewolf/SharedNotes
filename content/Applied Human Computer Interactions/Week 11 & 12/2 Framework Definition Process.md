#### 1 - Define the Form Factor, Posture, and Input Methods
* **Form Factor:** Designers determine the physical nature of the app. e.g. mobile app? website? etc.

#### 2 - Define Functional and Data Elements

*  **Data Objects:** These are the "things" the user interacts with, such as **photos, email messages, or customer records**
* **Functional Elements:** These are the **operations performed on data** and their visual representations. This includes tools to act on data and ways to structurally manage it
* **Assessment Criteria:** Designers return to context scenarios and persona goals to ensure solutions are appropriate

 A proposed solution should:
 *  **Accomplish user goals efficiently**
 *  Be a **best fit for design principles** and fit within **cost or technology parameters**
 * **Differentiate the interaction** from the competition and treat the product as if it were **human**

#### 3. Determine Functional Groups and Hierarchy

Once elements are identified, they must be organized logically to support user flow.

- **Real Estate and Containers:** Designers decide which elements need **large screen real estate** and which act as **containers** for other elements.
- **Flow and Sequence:** Elements are arranged to **optimize flow**, grouping items used together and following the **sequence** in which they will be used.
- **Mental Models:** Organization is heavily influenced by **interaction patterns** and the **persona's mental model** regarding what data they need to see at each decision point.

#### 4. Sketch the Interaction Framework

Designers create a visual "skeleton" of the product.

- **Method:** Framework sketches should be **simple**, starting with **rectangles and names**. They should describe the **relationships** between different functional areas.
- **Avoiding Detail:** While designers might visually hint at content, they must avoid the trap of designing **high-level detail** at this stage.
- **Example:** An early sketch for an online portal for **traveling nurses** might show general areas for "Jobs," "Housing," and "Map" without detailing specific job listings.

#### 5. Construct Key Path Scenarios

This step simulates the **primary way** a persona interacts with the product to reach their goal.

- **Storyboarding:** Designers use **storyboarding** and **iteration** to map out the interaction.
- **Non-linear Process:** Because goal achievement is rarely a straight line, these scenarios simulate **process variations** to ensure the design can handle different user paths.

####  6. Check Designs with Validation Scenarios

After the key path scenarios flow smoothly and the designer feels confident, they shift focus to less frequent interactions.

- **Alternative Scenarios:** Mapping different ways to reach the same goal.
- **Necessary-use Scenarios:** Interactions that **must be performed** even if they are infrequent.
- **Edge-case Scenarios:** Addressing **rare or extreme situations** that the system must still handle.

**Analogy for Understanding:** Defining a design framework is like **drafting a floor plan for a building**. You first decide the **form factor** (is it a skyscraper or a cottage?) and the **input methods** (stairs or elevators?). You then define the **functional elements** (kitchens, bathrooms) and **group them** so that the dining room is near the kitchen for better flow. You **sketch** the layout with simple boxes before you ever pick out the furniture (**high-level detail**) or test it against **validation scenarios**, such as how someone would evacuate during a fire drill (**edge-case scenario**).
